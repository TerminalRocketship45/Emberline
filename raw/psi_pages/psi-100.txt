Objectives
The objectives of this investigation were to:
(1) determine how rapidly a large scale fire grows in low-gravity and (2) investigate the low-g flammability
limits compared to those obtained in NASA’s normal gravity material flammability screening test.
Approach
The Saffire-III experiments were performed on the Orbital Science’s Cygnus resupply vehicle after it
deberthed from the ISS. As a follow on to the Saffire 1 investigation, the Saffire III investigation also used
SIBAL as the sample material. However, the experiments were conducted at a flow velocity of 30 cm/s
compared to 20 cm/s during Saffire I. The flow was either concurrent or opposed. Once the sample was
ignited flame images were captured using two cameras. In addition, thermocouples provided flow, flame, and
plume temperatures, two radiometers recorded flame radiation, solid state sensors were used to measure
pressure, CO2 and O2 mole faction, and two anemometer tracked flow velocity. All data was compressed,
and subsequently downlinked. Details related to samples and experimental parameters are provided in the
Experimental Details file in the Science document folder.
Hypothesis
This investigation hypothesizes that due to large differences between fire behavior in normal and reduced
gravity, the use of terrestrial fires and fire standards to design spacecraft fire safety systems presents an
inherent risk to the vehicle because of the significant level of uncertainty. While this approach has been
successful thus far, the uncertainty and risk will increase as exploration missions venture further from earth
with considerably longer transit times for a safe return. There is a significant lack of microgravity data
available for spacecraft designers on which to base their fire safety designs and procedures. To address this
knowledge gap, this investigation proposed to conduct large-scale fire safety experiments in an expendable
spacecraft without risk to a crew.
Research Impacts/Earth Benefits
The Saffire-I experiment provides a new way to study a realistic fire on an exploration vehicle, which has not
been possible in the past because the risks are too high. Instruments on an unmanned cargo vessel
measure oxygen, carbon dioxide, heat, pressure and flame growth, and two video cameras provide views of
the flame. Results determine microgravity flammability limits for several spacecraft materials, help to validate
NASA’s material selection criteria, and how microgravity and limited oxygen affect flame size. The
